Tutorial

A short guide to running your first prediction with PPB4 and making sense of the results.

On this page

1. Overview 2. Your first prediction 3. Reading the results 4. Choosing a fingerprint 5. Active vs. inactive 6. Filtering by target type 7. Similar known compounds 8. Predicting multiple compounds 9. Sharing results 10. Citing PPB4

1. Overview

PPB4 is a target-prediction tool for small molecules. Given a chemical structure, it estimates which protein targets the molecule is most likely to be active against, and which targets it is most likely to be inactive against. Predictions are produced by multitask deep neural networks trained on bioactivity data from ChEMBL 35.

Compared to its predecessor PPB3, PPB4 introduces three changes worth noting up front: (a) it now predicts confirmed inactivity in addition to activity, (b) it is trained on a substantially larger dataset (~5.8 M compound–target interactions), and (c) it adds the MAP4 fingerprint, which is particularly well suited to peptides and other macrocyclic molecules.

2. Your first prediction

Open the homepage and follow these steps:

  • Provide a structure. Either draw your molecule in the JSME editor or paste a SMILES string into the Current SMILES box. The drawing and the SMILES box stay synchronised: editing one updates the other.
  • Try the example. If you just want to see the tool in action, click Load example to fill in the test compound (O=C(NO)c4ccc(CN3c1cc(C(F)(F)F)ccc1S(=O)(=O)CC2CCCCC23)cc4) and run it through the models to see which targets PPB4 predicts it hits, and which it predicts it does not.
  • Pick a model. Consensus is the recommended choice: it combines all four fingerprint models. See section 4 below for guidance.
  • Choose how many targets to predict. The slider goes from 5 to 50; 20 is usually a good starting point.
  • Click Predict targets. A new browser tab opens at a unique results URL such as ppb4.gdb.tools/result/<id> and renders the predictions.

Predictions usually finish in under five seconds for a single compound.

3. Reading the results

Each compound you submit is shown as its own card on the results page. Within each card you will see three tabs:

  • Combined predictions (default): active and inactive predictions merged into one ranked list, sorted by raw confidence descending. A coloured pill on each row indicates whether the prediction comes from the active or the inactive model. If the same target appears in both rankings, it is shown twice, once as active and once as inactive, with its respective confidence scores.
  • Active predictions: the top targets ranked by P(active) only.
  • Inactive predictions: the top targets ranked by P(inactive) only.

Each row contains the target name (linked to its ChEMBL report card), the ChEMBL ID, the target type and protein class, the organism, and a confidence score between 0 and 1. The confidence is the raw output of the corresponding neural network and should be read as a relative ranking signal rather than as a calibrated probability.

4. Choosing a fingerprint

PPB4 offers four fingerprint-based models. They all map your molecule to a 4096-bit vector that the neural network consumes, but they capture chemistry differently:

Fingerprint What it encodes
ECFP4 Circular substructures around each atom, up to a radius of two bonds.
AtomPair Pairs of atoms together with the topological distance between them.
Layered An RDKit path-based fingerprint that captures substructure layering.
MAP4 MinHash-encoded atom pairs with their surrounding circular substructures.

Which one to use

Start with Consensus. It runs all four models and keeps, for each target, the highest score any of them gave, so a target only needs to be recognised by one fingerprint to surface. Each row on the results page shows which fingerprint produced its score.

Running the same compound through two fingerprints is a good way to gauge robustness: targets that appear in both rankings are more trustworthy.

5. Active vs. inactive predictions

Most target-prediction tools answer one question: which targets is my compound likely to bind? PPB4 also answers a second: which targets is it likely to be inactive against?

The two answers come from two separate networks: one trained on confirmed active interactions, one on confirmed inactive ones. They share the input fingerprint but have independent output layers over different, partly overlapping target sets, so their rankings can disagree on the same target.

6. Filtering by target type

ChEMBL targets cover more than single proteins: cell lines, whole organisms, protein complexes, protein families and protein–protein interactions are all included. The Target type dropdown above each results table lets you focus on one category at a time, and its counts reflect what is actually present in the current top-N list.

7. Similar known compounds

A prediction on its own does not tell you why the model made it. Every row in the results table carries a button that answers that question: it shows the compounds already annotated against that target in the training data, ranked by how similar they are to your query.

The search is restricted to that one target and that one side of the data, so a row from the active list is compared against known actives for that target, and a row from the inactive list against known inactives. Similarity is the Tanimoto coefficient on the same fingerprint used for the prediction, and the ten closest compounds are returned.

Each neighbour shows its ChEMBL ID, linked to its compound report card, its structure, and its similarity to your query. A prediction backed by a close neighbour rests on chemistry the model has seen before. The panel has its own Export CSV button.

8. Predicting multiple compounds

Switch to the Paste SMILES tab on the homepage to submit a batch. Enter one SMILES per line: comments, blank lines, and identifiers are not supported, so keep the input clean. Each compound becomes its own card on the results page, each with its own filters and tabs.

9. Sharing results

Each prediction is saved server-side and served at a unique URL of the form ppb4.gdb.tools/result/<id>. You can copy that URL from the browser's address bar and share it with colleagues: anyone with the link will see exactly the same predictions, filters and tabs included.

The Export CSV button at the top of the results page saves the predictions as a spreadsheet. It writes one row per predicted target, covering every compound in the batch and all three tabs, with the ChEMBL ID, target name, type, class, organism and confidence score of each.
